Article XXI(b)(iii) of the GATT 1994 permits a Member to take *"any action which it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests"* taken *"in time of war or other emergency in international relations."* 

For seventy years, the chapeau's phrase *"which it considers necessary"* was treated by many states as rendering the clause entirely self-judging — immune from panel review. That assumption has collapsed since 2019, and the tariff escalations of 2025 have pushed the doctrine back into the center of WTO litigation.

---

## The Foundational Case: *Russia – Traffic in Transit*

The turning point was *Russia – Measures Concerning Traffic in Transit* (DS512), the first panel ever to adjudicate Article XXI on the merits. Russia, with the United States appearing as a third party, argued the provision was non-justiciable. 

The panel rejected that argument, though it ultimately found Russia's actions consistent with the exception, and established several interpretive principles along the way:

* **Multilateral Integrity:** Treating Article XXI as a mere unilateral condition subject to a Member's own will would be entirely contrary to the security and predictability of the multilateral trading system.
* **Objective Fit:** For a measure to fall within Article XXI(b), it must objectively satisfy one of the three enumerated subparagraphs (i), (ii), or (iii).
* **The Good Faith Obligation:** The panel read an inherent good-faith requirement into the chapeau. Members may not invoke Article XXI as a means of circumventing their substantive GATT obligations.

The clause was thus bifurcated: objectively reviewable as to whether a measure fits within subparagraphs (i), (ii), or (iii), but subject to a margin of deference on whether the underlying interest is genuinely "essential."

---

## Section 232 and the 2018–2022 Litigation Wave

That framework was immediately tested by the first Trump administration's 2018 Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%), challenged by the EU, China, Turkey, Switzerland, Norway, Mexico, Canada, India, Russia, and others (*DS544 and consolidated disputes*). 

Panels found the duties inconsistent with Article II:1 because they exceeded the United States' bound tariff rates, and found the exemptions granted to select trading partners inconsistent with the most-favoured-nation obligation under Article I:1. Applying the *Russia* test, the panels concluded the Section 232 measures did not objectively fit within Article XXI(b)'s enumerated categories. A parallel panel ruled against the United States in the dispute brought by Norway on the same measures.

The United States refused to accept these outcomes. In January 2023, it announced it would appeal every adverse Section 232 panel report — into an Appellate Body it has blocked from being reconstituted since 2019. Because the Appellate Body remains without a quorum, that decision has effectively suspended the disputes indefinitely, leaving the United States under no operative obligation to withdraw or adjust the tariffs. 

Washington simultaneously challenged the *Russia* precedent on a normative level, stating its intent to raise the justiciability question in WTO dispute-settlement reform talks and to seek an authoritative interpretation of Article XXI, on the premise that Members must "clarify and adopt a shared understanding" of the exception. A 2025 Chinese government compliance report describes this "appeal into the void" tactic as a deliberate strategy to keep unfavorable rulings from ever taking legal effect.

---

## Retaliation Turns the Exception Against Its Users

A second wrinkle emerged once retaliating states themselves invoked Article XXI. In *China – Additional Duties on Certain Products from the United States* (DS558), decided in August 2024, a panel found China's retaliatory tariffs — imposed in response to the U.S. steel and aluminum duties — inconsistent with WTO rules, marking the first panel win for the United States after four consecutive losses on its own Section 232 defense. 

The ruling confirmed that the *Russia* good-faith and objective-fit tests cut both ways: a Member cannot bootstrap a retaliatory measure into Article XXI merely because the originating measure was itself framed in security terms.

---

## The 2025 Escalation

The pattern has intensified rather than resolved. New steel and aluminum proclamations imposed a 25% duty effective March 12, 2025, echoing the 2018 measures and again invoking Section 232's national-security rationale, prompting renewed threats of EU and other retaliatory action. 

With the Appellate Body still non-functional, each new round of tariffs and counter-tariffs generates panel findings that are fully litigated but never legally consummated — a cycle that has now stretched across three U.S. administrations and a growing docket of sequential disputes, all while the underlying legal question — how far a self-judging clause can be stretched before it swallows the rules it was meant to except from — remains formally unresolved at the systemic level.

---

## The Takeaway

For practitioners, three propositions from this jurisprudence are now reasonably settled:

1. **Bifurcated Justiciability:** Article XXI(b) is justiciable as to objective fit with subparagraphs (i)–(iii), even though the "essential security interest" determination itself retains a margin of deference.
2. **Mutual Good-Faith Constraints:** A good-faith requirement constrains both the invoking state and any state that attempts to retaliate in kind under the same banner.
3. **Appellate Stage Paralysis:** Procedural gridlock at the appellate stage — not substantive doctrine — is now the primary obstacle to enforcement. 

Counsel advising on security-based trade measures should therefore treat panel-stage success as necessary but not sufficient, and build compliance strategy around the reality that adverse rulings may sit unenforced indefinitely.
