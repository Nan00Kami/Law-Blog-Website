As sovereign states accelerate decarbonization targets, regulatory interventions—ranging from early coal and gas phase-outs to retroactive adjustments of renewable feed-in tariffs (FiTs)—are increasingly clashing with investor protections in international investment agreements (IIAs). 

At the center of this friction lies the contested boundary between compensable **indirect expropriation** and non-compensable **legitimate police powers**, alongside the scope of legitimate expectations under the **Fair and Equitable Treatment (FET)** standard.

---

## 1. The Legal Tension: Indirect Expropriation vs. Police Powers

Indirect expropriation occurs when a state measure permanently deprives an investor of the economic use, benefit, or control of an investment without formal transfer of legal title. However, customary international law recognizes the doctrine of police powers: non-discriminatory regulatory measures adopted in good faith to protect public welfare, the environment, or human health do not constitute expropriation and do not warrant compensation.

Tribunals historically apply two competing doctrines to adjudicate this boundary:

* **The Sole Effects Doctrine:** Focused exclusively on the economic impact of the state measure on the investor. If the deprivation is total or substantial, compensation is due regardless of public interest intent.
* **The Proportionality Standard:** Favored in modern jurisprudence, balancing the public objective against the severity of the investor's economic deprivation (as observed in *Tecmed v. Mexico* and reaffirmed in modern treaty texts like CETA).

Under modern arbitral standards, for a climate regulation to qualify as a non-compensable police power, the state must establish that the measure was non-discriminatory, adopted in accordance with due process, and proportionate to the environmental harm addressed.

## 2. FET and Legitimate Expectations: Lessons from the Renewable Energy Disputes

The wave of arbitrations arising from European renewable energy reforms (e.g., *Charanne v. Spain*, *Eiser v. Spain*, *Novenergia v. Spain*) provides the foundational test for state regulatory flexibility. 

Claimants argued that statutory incentive regimes created binding commitments that states could not unilaterally dismantle without violating the FET standard under the Energy Charter Treaty (ECT). The resulting awards established critical baseline rules:

1. **No Absolute Regulatory Freeze:** Tribunals broadly agree that absent an explicit stabilization clause or specific, formal commitments addressed directly to the investor, states retain the sovereign prerogative to amend general regulatory and economic policies.
2. **The Proportionality and Reasonableness Threshold:** While states may amend regulatory schemes, fundamental or retroactive alterations that completely undermine the economic rationale of an investment breach FET (*Novenergia*). 
3. **The Diligent Investor Standard:** An investor’s expectations are only protected if they were reasonable and based on objective due diligence at the time the capital was committed.

## 3. The Frontline: Early Fossil Fuel Phase-Outs

The doctrine is now being tested in reverse: traditional energy companies initiating ISDS claims against mandatory fossil fuel phase-outs (exemplified by claims against coal bans in Germany and the Netherlands under the ECT, such as *RWE v. Netherlands* and *Uniper v. Netherlands*).

Claimants argue that statutory dates requiring plant decommissioning prior to the conclusion of their technical operating lifespans amount to direct destruction of commercial value. 

In response, host states increasingly assert:
* **The Precautionary Principle and International Obligations:** Domestic phase-outs operationalize binding international treaty commitments under the Paris Agreement.
* **Foreseeability of Climate Action:** Given decades of international climate negotiations and tightening emission regimes, a prudent investor operating after 2015 could not maintain a reasonable expectation of unconstrained fossil fuel extraction or combustion.

---

## 4. Key Takeaways for Practitioners

* **Specific Commitments Matter:** General statutory targets rarely generate actionable legitimate expectations. Claims hinge almost entirely on explicit, tailored representations or stabilization covenants granted by the state.
* **Carve-Outs in Modern IIAs:** Newer bilateral and plurilateral treaties explicitly affirm the host state’s right to regulate for climate and environmental objectives, insulating non-discriminatory environmental measures from indirect expropriation claims.
* **Forum Reassessment:** With mass withdrawals from the Energy Charter Treaty by European jurisdictions, investors and host states alike must navigate jurisdictional hurdles, intra-EU arbitration objections, and shifting enforcement avenues outside European domestic courts.
