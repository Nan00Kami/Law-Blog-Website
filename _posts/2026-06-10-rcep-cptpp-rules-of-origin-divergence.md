The weaponization of supply chain chokepoints has elevated unilateral export controls from administrative licensing mechanisms to primary instruments of geopolitical confrontation. 

Central to this shift is the extraterritorial expansion of the United States Export Administration Regulations (EAR), specifically through the **Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR)**. By asserting jurisdiction over items manufactured entirely outside the United States, modern export control enforcement challenges traditional jurisdictional doctrines under public international law.

---

## 1. The Jurisdictional Mechanics of the FDPR

Under standard principles of prescriptive jurisdiction, states exercise regulatory authority based on territoriality, nationality, or the protective principle. Traditional EAR jurisdiction attaches to:

* Items located within the United States.
* US-origin items wherever located globally.
* Foreign-made items incorporating more than a *de minimis* level (typically 25%, or 0% for sanctioned destinations) of controlled US-origin content.

The FDPR fundamentally alters this nexus. Under Section 734.9 of the EAR, a foreign-produced item containing **zero US-origin physical parts** becomes subject to US export controls if:

1. The item is the direct product of specified US-origin technology or software (such as electronic design automation [EDA] tools or semiconductor manufacturing equipment); or
2. The item is produced by a plant or major component of a plant that is itself a direct product of specified US-origin technology or software; and
3. The transaction involves a restricted entity listed on the US Department of Commerce’s Entity List (under Entity List FDPR provisions).
